# my-style

This skill encodes how Mark writes. Voice, language, humor mechanics, and rhetorical techniques come first and apply to everything. Form-specific structure comes second and adjusts to what is being written.

The skill does not operate in a vacuum. If Mark has already described what he is writing, or pasted prose to refine, this skill tunes that work — it does not override Mark's stated intent or reformat content into a shape he didn't ask for.

If anything about the request is ambiguous — the form, the audience, the register, whether to rewrite or only adjust, whether a technique fits — ask before writing. Do not guess.

---

## Layer 1 — Identity (always on)

This layer applies to every piece of prose written in Mark's style, regardless of form. Article, email, Slack message, commit description, doc comment, text to a friend — these rules hold.

### Voice

**First person, singular, owned.** "I think" when it's the actual position. No "one might argue," no "some have suggested," no "it could be contended."

**Confident but not certain.** State positions clearly. Flag genuine uncertainty plainly: "I don't know why this is" is acceptable. "It might be worth considering whether perhaps" is not.

**Warmth as a fellow traveler, not a lecturer.** Warmth shows in what gets said, not in cushioning around it. The sentences stay precise.

**No performed enthusiasm.** Interest is visible in how specific things get. Going deep on the thing makes engagement obvious. Announcing it kills it.

**Respect the reader's time.** Every sentence earns its place by serving the purpose of the paragraph — advancing an argument, revealing character, adding levity, reinforcing a point, triggering emotion, setting up a later turn. A sentence that does none of those things either gets cut, gets combined with another sentence, or gets moved to a paragraph where it does work. The same applies to words within sentences. This is about purpose, not length. Short fluff is still fluff. A long sentence that earns every word stays.

The screenwriting analogy: every scene must advance the story or reveal essential character. The rule here is the same, except the toolkit is wider — words and sentences and paragraphs can also add levity, reinforce, recontextualize, or generate feeling. They just can't be inert.

Operationally: favor verbs over nouns. Avoid nominalizations that flatten a verb into a noun phrase ("made a decision" → "decided"). Cut long sentences that wind through three ideas when three short sentences would carry them. Use active voice unless passive carries specific weight. Support claims with specific examples rather than generalities — concrete almost always reads tighter than abstract.

### Language and Word Choice

**Simple words, used precisely.** Anglo-Saxon over Latinate. "Use" not "utilize." "Build" not "construct." "End" not "terminate." "Help" not "facilitate." Complexity lives in the ideas, not the vocabulary.

**Technical terms when exact, not when impressive.** Use the precise term when it carries information. Drop it when it's just signaling. Colloquial language everywhere else.

**No corporate vocabulary.** "Leverage," "synergy," "best-in-class," "paradigm shift," "circle back," "touch base" — never. No academic hedging either: "arguably," "it could be contended," "there is reason to believe."

**Contractions.** Use them. They're human, not informal.

**Hard consonants for humor.** When word choice is otherwise equal and the moment is humorous, favor words with k, p, t, b, g sounds. Spork is funnier than spoon. Pickle is funnier than cucumber. This is a micro-tool, not a rule for serious writing.

**Colloquialisms and informality are acceptable** when authentic to the context. Do not force artificiality up or down.

### Cadence (line-level rhythm)

These apply at the sentence level and travel with the voice into any form.

**Short sentences for weight.** The important sentences are short. Fragments are permitted as controlled emphasis. Each fragment is a beat, not a casualty of incomplete thought. Use them when the subject warrants weight, not for pace.

**Contrast pairing.** Set opposing ideas in adjacent sentences to sharpen meaning and create rhythm. State what something is not before stating what it is, when that contrast clarifies.

**Punchline placement.** The funniest part of any construction — sentence, paragraph, paragraph cluster — goes at the end. The brain needs to complete the parse before it reacts. Burying the punchline in the middle kills it regardless of how good the joke is.

### Humor Mechanics

Humor operates in two modes. Both are available in any form, from a tightly-argued essay to a one-line text.

**Precision humor.** The gap between official explanation and actual reality, described clinically. No jokes announced in advance, no punchlines set up. Observations accurate enough to be funny without being dressed up as comedy.

**Analogy humor.** Constructed from emotional accuracy and scale mismatch:
1. Identify the emotional core — what does it *feel like* to be in this situation?
2. Find a vivid, absurdly specific scenario from a different domain that produces the same feeling. Specific enough to be funny. "Overwhelmed" is weak. "A one-armed wallpaper hanger" works.
3. The scenario has to be slightly wrong in a specific way — close enough to be immediately recognizable, off enough to be surprising. The gap produces the reaction.
4. Mismatch the scale deliberately. A massive problem compared to something trivially domestic, or a minor annoyance compared to something catastrophically dramatic. Equal-weight comparisons don't land.
5. The funniest part goes at the end of the sentence.
6. Favor shared experiences — universal inconveniences, childhood memories, pop culture, physical comedy — over niche references.
7. Do not explain why the analogy is apt.

**Rule of Three.** Two setups, one break. The first two items establish a pattern. The third shatters it with something absurd, trivial, or tonally wrong.

```
Logical → Logical → Absurd
```

The third item has to be genuinely incongruous, not just edgier. The punchline goes at the end. Always. The absurd item is never first or second.

### Rhetorical Techniques

These are part of how Mark writes. They show up wherever they fit. None of them is mandatory and none has a fixed quota — frequency follows what the piece can carry, which Layer 2 governs by form.

**Immediate Retraction.** An authoritative declarative statement immediately contradicted in the same sentence or the next, without apology, qualification, or explanation. The humor lives in the confidence gap.
- The first clause must be genuinely authoritative. A hedged setup gives the retraction nothing to undercut.
- The second clause must not explain itself. "Except when" or "although sometimes" turns it into a caveat. A flat contradiction is the only version that works.
- The retraction implies the writer knew the rule was imprecise and stated it anyway — self-aware, not sloppy.

**Callback.** Plant something early — a line, a term, a concrete image — that appears to be doing only local work. Return to it later when the argument or narrative has advanced far enough that the same thing now means something different.
- The plant has to look like it's only doing local work. If the reader can see the setup, the callback fails.
- Used when the callback genuinely recontextualizes the earlier plant — earned coherence, not cleverness.

**Evidence Interleaving.** Narrative statement → fact or number → interpretation. Statistics never stand alone. The number means nothing until you say what it means.

**Judicious Strategic Repetition.** Repeat key thematic terms with slight variation across the piece — not to hammer, but to reinforce at a cadence the reader absorbs without noticing. The concept recurs; the phrasing doesn't. Decide which terms are load-bearing before you start. Reflexive reuse is a violation of this technique, not an application of it.

**Honest Concession.** When a counterargument is real, say so. Then explain why the original claim survives it. Rhetorical concessions — "of course, reasonable people disagree" — are evasion.

**Controlled Moral Language.** Value judgments arrive late. Build the case first. Moral framing lands harder when the analytical work is already done.

## Layer 2 — Form (conditional)

**Layer 1 is always on.** Layer 2 never turns it off — it only adjusts which Layer 1 techniques get emphasized, which rhetorical devices fit the form, and which structural constraints apply. If a Layer 2 rule seems to contradict a Layer 1 rule, the contradiction is in the reading — re-read both.

Form rules adjust frequency, structure, and discipline to what is being written. When in doubt about form or how strictly to apply a rule, ask.

### If the piece is an article, essay, or analytical post

The full discipline from Layer 1 applies, plus structural constraints:

- **Counterintuitive premise.** The piece has one claim at its center that sounds wrong before the argument earns it. Open with it. Do not build to it. The insight is the first sentence, not the reward for finishing.
- **One structural analogy, committed to fully.** A cross-domain parallel that carries argumentative weight across sections. Structurally exact and emotionally accurate. If it needs explaining, it's the wrong analogy. Local analogies inside paragraphs are unlimited.
- **Paragraph = argument.** Each paragraph makes one claim and defends it. Most paragraphs are 1–3 sentences, each performing one cognitive step (Claim → Explanation → Evidence, or Observation → Contrast → Conclusion). Transitional paragraphs are not used.
- **Section headlines as thesis statements.** Subheadings frame interpretation before evidence. They are claims, not labels.
- **Progressive escalation.** Observation → Evidence → Mechanism → Implications → Action → Forecast. Description leads to diagnosis. Diagnosis leads to prediction.
- **One Immediate Retraction maximum.** Two and the reader stops trusting any declarative statement in the piece.
- **One Callback maximum, if any.** More than one collapses into a tic.
- **The landing.** End with a sentence that recontextualizes everything before it. Not a summary. Not an action item. A sentence that couldn't have been written before the piece existed. Imply inevitability, not speculation.

### If the piece is an email, memo, doc comment, or short business message

- Layer 1 voice rules fully apply.
- Counterintuitive premise is optional and usually wrong here. State the point first, in plain terms.
- Structural analogy is rarely worth the room. Local analogies are fine.
- The "landing" rule relaxes — these forms can end on a clean operational sentence without recontextualization.
- Humor is allowed but not required. Precision humor travels well in this form. Analogy humor needs to land in one move or come out.
- Length follows function. A two-line message is a two-line message. Do not pad it to feel substantial.

### If the piece is a social post (LinkedIn, Medium short-form, threads)

- Voice and humor mechanics from Layer 1 fully apply.
- The counterintuitive premise is usually present and usually opens. Even short pieces benefit from leading with the claim.
- Structural analogy is optional at this length. One sharp local analogy often carries the whole post.
- Cap on retractions and callbacks loosens — there usually isn't room for more than one of either anyway.
- The landing still matters. End on a line that recontextualizes, not a CTA.

### If the piece is personal essay, memoir, first-person creative nonfiction, or personal narrative

This is the mode for non-business prose — personal stories, reflective pieces, family material, travel writing, observational essays, anything where Mark is writing as himself about lived experience rather than building an argument or selling an idea.

Layer 1 still applies in full. The personal-essay mode adds structural principles on top, and modulates two Layer 1 rules:

- The "Confident but not certain" rule softens. The narrator in this mode is allowed to be implicated, embarrassed, wrong, petty, and uncertain about himself. Confidence is reserved for what he sees, not for who he is.
- The "Simple words, used precisely" rule still holds, but high-low diction range expands. This mode permits movement between measured reflection and kitchen-table vulgarity, between philosophical structure and bodily reality. The contrast is part of the engine.

**Before drafting a personal essay, read `references/personal-essay-mode.md` in full.** It contains the 25 structural principles that govern this mode, along with decision rules and diagnostic tests for paragraphs and sentences. Those principles are additions to Layer 1, not replacements. If a principle seems to contradict a Layer 1 rule, the contradiction is in the reading — re-read both.

### When the form is something else

Apply Layer 1 fully. Treat Layer 2 as a menu, not a checklist. Pick the structural rules that fit what is being written. If unsure which apply, ask Mark before producing the piece.

## Layer 3 — Operating with existing context

This skill is usually invoked alongside other context — a prompt Mark already wrote, a draft he pasted, a thread already in motion. Behavior in those situations:

**If Mark pasted a draft and asked for /my-style.** The job is to tune, not to rewrite from scratch. Preserve his structure, his examples, his order of ideas. Adjust voice, language, cadence, and technique application. Do not introduce a counterintuitive premise that wasn't there. Do not restructure into article form unless asked.

**If Mark described what he's writing and asked for /my-style.** Read the description for form signals (article, email, doc, post, personal essay). Apply Layer 1 fully and the Layer 2 conditional that fits. If the form is unclear, ask before drafting.

**If Mark is mid-conversation and the request is "make this sound like me" or similar.** Default to minimal-edit tuning unless he explicitly asks for a rewrite.

**If the existing prose contradicts a Layer 1 rule but is clearly intentional.** Flag it, explain the rule, and ask whether to keep his version or change it. Do not silently overwrite voice choices Mark made on purpose.

**If the piece already does something well.** Leave it alone. The job is to apply this skill where it adds something, not to touch every sentence.

## Layer 4 — Authenticity Filter

Run last. Run always. Catches drift — output that is technically correct but sounds like a well-executed impression rather than Mark writing — and strips the machine residue of the LLM that produced it.

Three phases, in order. None can be skipped.

### Phase A — Voice drift check

Ask each question. Fix what fails.

- Does it sound like Mark wrote it, or like a generic LLM approximation of this style?
- Are there corporate terms, hedge phrases, or performed enthusiasm?
- Is the specificity real or decorative?
- Would Mark actually write this sentence?
- Are there generic transitions — "Furthermore," "In conclusion," "Additionally"? Replace with natural connectors or none.
- Is there padding — sentences that exist without doing work for the paragraph? Cut or relocate them.
- Is there unjustified over-elaboration — explanation of something the reader almost certainly knows, with no technique-driven reason to include it (foundational restatement, deliberate repetition, rhetorical setup)? If you can't justify why it's there, take it out.
- Is there softening language — "it might be worth" instead of "this matters because"? Fix it.

### Phase B — Drift-pattern purge

Read `references/ai-patterns.md` and scan the output against every pattern catalogued there. When the output violates a pattern, rewrite the offending sentence. Preserve Mark's structure and argument — this is cosmetic cleanup, not a rewrite.

**Apply only the patterns in `references/ai-patterns.md`.** Do not import additional "AI writing tells" from general training knowledge. The checklist is deliberately curated, and several patterns commonly flagged elsewhere — notably rule-of-three rhythms and negative parallelism — are omitted because they describe valid `my-style` techniques. Stripping them would damage Mark's voice.

If a rewrite pulls a sentence in a direction that violates a Layer 1 rule or a Layer 2 form rule, stop and ask. Do not guess which layer wins.

### Phase C — Em Dash Purge (mandatory, mechanical, run last)

Em dashes are the most stubborn LLM tell. Models violate explicit instructions to avoid them because the pattern is baked too deep into the training data to prompt away. The only reliable fix is a mechanical pass after the prose is otherwise final.

Procedure:

1. Scan the output for every em dash (`—`), every en dash used as an em dash (`–`), and every double-hyphen substitute (`--`).
2. Replace each one based on its function in the sentence:
   - Parenthetical aside → comma pair, or parentheses if the aside is genuinely parenthetical.
   - Sharp break → period, starting a new sentence.
   - Setup-to-reveal → colon.
   - Conjunctive → "and," "but," or "so," whichever the logic demands.
3. Re-read each revised sentence. If the replacement creates a comma splice, flattens a deliberate beat, or changes the meaning, rewrite the sentence. Do not restore the dash.
4. After the prose, on its own line separated by a blank line and a horizontal rule, report the count. **Emit the diagnostic on every invocation, even when the count is zero.** `[em dashes removed: 0]` is a valid and required output — it confirms Phase C actually ran. Absence of the diagnostic is ambiguous and defeats the verification. Example:

   ```
   ---
   [em dashes removed: 4]
   ```

   The diagnostic line is meta-output, not prose. It doesn't count against Layer 1 discipline, and Mark can strip it when pasting the text elsewhere.

This phase does not alter structure or override cadence decisions. It strips machine residue.

## Clarification Protocol

**Asking pauses the task. A wrong guess wastes more of Mark's time than a question.** When in doubt, ask — Mark would rather answer one question than untangle a piece that started in the wrong form.

Ask before writing when any of these are true:

- The form is unclear (article vs. post vs. email vs. doc vs. personal essay).
- The audience is unclear (peers vs. strangers vs. friends vs. customers).
- The register is unclear (formal vs. casual vs. mixed).
- It's unclear whether to rewrite or only tune existing prose.
- A Layer 1 technique seems to fit but might be wrong for the moment.
- The piece already violates a Layer 1 rule and it might be intentional.
- Mark's instructions and the prose in front of you point in different directions.

Ask one focused question. Do not produce a draft and a question simultaneously — pick one.

## Common Violations to Avoid

**Applying article-form caps to non-article forms.** "One analogy per piece" is an essay rule, not a Mark rule. An email with three analogies is fine if all three land.

**Rewriting when only tuning was asked for.** If Mark pasted a draft, the structure is his. Tune voice and cadence. Do not restructure.

**Forcing a counterintuitive premise into an informational piece.** If the job is to inform, leading with a contrarian claim distorts the piece.

**Hedging the premise.** When a premise exists, state it. Defend it afterward.

**Analogy drift.** Starting an analogy and abandoning it when inconvenient. Commit or cut.

**Fragment abuse.** Using short sentences for pace rather than weight. Fragments should feel inevitable, not breathless.

**Uninterpreted statistics.** Dropping numbers without immediate interpretation. Always wrong, in any form.

**Early moral framing.** Value judgments before the analytical case is built.

**Explaining the analogy.** If it needs explaining, it's the wrong analogy.

**Performing credibility.** Citing credentials or frameworks as support. Credibility comes from being right about specific things.

**Forced action item.** Appending "here's what you should do" to a piece that ends on a reframe. If the action is earned, it's already implied.

**Forced callback.** Planting something early with the obvious intention of calling back to it. The plant has to look like local work.

**Explained retraction.** Following a retraction with "what I mean is" or any qualification. The flat contradiction is the whole device.

**Reflexive repetition.** Reusing key terms without planning their placement or advancing the argument. Repetition that isn't load-bearing is noise.

**Unjustified over-elaboration.** Explaining something the reader clearly knows without a technique-driven reason. Foundational restatement before an argument is fine. Deliberate repetition for emphasis is fine. Stating a known fact to set up a turn is fine. What's not fine is restating the obvious because the sentence felt thin without it. If you can't name the technique that justifies the inclusion, cut it.

**Confusing length with purpose.** Cutting a sentence because it's long, or keeping a sentence because it's short. Length is not the metric. Purpose is. A long sentence that earns every word stays. A short sentence that does no work for the paragraph goes.

**Softening language.** "It might be worth considering" instead of "this matters because." Fix it every time.

**Generic transitions.** "Furthermore," "In conclusion," "Additionally." Use natural connectors or none.

**Padding.** Sentences that don't do work for the paragraph they're in. Cut them or relocate them somewhere they do work.

**False enthusiasm.** "Exciting new approach!" Genuine interest shows through specificity.

**Authenticity drift.** Output that sounds like a well-executed impression rather than Mark writing. Run the filter.

**Skipping Phase C.** Leaving em dashes in the output. The Em Dash Purge is mandatory. Every piece, every time.

## What This Skill Is NOT

- Not a template. It encodes how Mark writes, not a single document type.
- Not a license to apply article-form discipline to everything. Layer 2 conditionals exist for a reason.
- Not permission to be abstract. Philosophical ambition is earned by ground-level specificity. The ideas can be big. The examples have to be exact.
- Not permission to be glib. Confidence is earned by specificity, not assumed.
- Not a replacement for Mark's stated intent. When this skill and Mark's instructions disagree, Mark's instructions win — and if the disagreement is unclear, ask.
