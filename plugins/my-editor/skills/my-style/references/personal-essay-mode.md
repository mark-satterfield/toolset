# Personal Essay Mode

The 25 principles below govern Mark's personal essay, memoir, and first-person creative nonfiction writing. They are additions to `my-style` Layer 1, not replacements. Every Layer 1 rule still applies: no corporate vocabulary, contractions, precision humor, the named rhetorical techniques (Immediate Retraction, Callback, Evidence Interleaving, Judicious Strategic Repetition, Honest Concession, Controlled Moral Language), the humor mechanics (comedic Rule of Three, analogy humor, hard consonants), and the cadence rules.

SKILL.md specifies two modulations to Layer 1 for this mode:

- "Confident but not certain" softens. The narrator is allowed to be implicated, embarrassed, wrong, petty, and uncertain about himself. Confidence is reserved for what he sees, not for who he is.
- "Simple words, used precisely" expands its diction range. High-low contrast is part of the engine.

Everything else in Layer 1 stacks on top of the principles below. Where a principle seems to contradict Layer 1, re-read both — the contradiction is almost always in the reading.

---

## The 25 principles

**1. Write from a positioned self.**
The narrator is never neutral. He is present, implicated, biased, embarrassed, observant, and not entirely admirable. Do not write from a floating intelligence. Write from a person with habits, irritations, blind spots, preferences, and social damage. The "I" is not there to confess nobly. The "I" is there to notice, misread, judge, expose, and occasionally indict himself.

**2. Begin with the ordinary, not the important.**
Start with something small enough to seem harmless: a trip, a phone call, a family habit, a store clerk, a bodily inconvenience, a misunderstanding, a sentence someone said, a household object, a social ritual. Do not begin with theme. Begin with incident. Trust that the large idea will emerge from the small thing being examined hard enough.

**3. Treat every small event as a delivery system for a larger truth.**
A minor inconvenience is never just a minor inconvenience. It is evidence. Use the surface event to reveal something underneath: family structure, vanity, loneliness, class, affection, shame, aging, national absurdity, etiquette, power, self-delusion. Do not announce the meaning. Let the sentence structure reveal it.

**4. Prefer observation over explanation.**
Do not explain what kind of person someone is if a single behavior will reveal it. Do not say "He was inconsiderate." Write the thing he did, the way he held the cup, the way he paused too long, the phrase he used, the object he brought, the timing of the insult. Specific behavior carries more force than summary.

**5. Be exact where most people are vague.**
Whenever a sentence starts to become generic, force it into physical reality. Replace soft words with concrete ones: not "bad language" but the exact phrase, not "drunk" but what happened to the body, not "awkward" but what each person did with their hands, eyes, or silence, not "messy" but the stain, the odor, the broken latch, the damp paper towel. Precision is not decorative. It is the engine.

**6. Make the sentence do the turn.**
A large share of the humor and force should come from the sentence changing direction. Build sentences that begin in reason and end in insult, begin in dignity and end in filth, begin in sentiment and end in embarrassment, begin in abstraction and end in something bodily or humiliating. Do not bolt the joke onto the sentence. Make the sentence itself pivot.

**7. Put pressure on the final clause.**
The end of the sentence should often be the hardest, cleanest, most irreversible version of the thought. The final words should reverse what preceded them, narrow it, contaminate it, literalize it, or expose the true motive beneath it. A sentence should often feel one way until its ending tells you what it really was.

**8. Deliver outrageous material in a calm voice.**
Do not strain for comic emphasis. Do not signal that a line is funny. State the sharpest, strangest, or most indecent thing in the tone of plain reportage. The less the prose performs the joke, the more the sentence lands.

**9. Keep intelligence visible but unshowy.**
The voice should be perceptive without sounding pleased with itself. Do not sound literary in a grand way. Do not sound academic. Do not sound as if the narrator is auditioning his wit. The intelligence should appear in selection of detail, timing of reversal, quality of analogy, and the exact pressure applied to a sentence.

**10. Let self-exposure stay exposed.**
When the narrator looks petty, vain, weak, delusional, cruel, frightened, needy, or ridiculous, do not rescue him. Do not immediately soften, justify, redeem, or moralize. Embarrassment is evidence. Leave it on the page long enough to matter.

**11. Let judgment and self-judgment live side by side.**
The narrator should notice what is absurd in other people, but he must also understand that he belongs to the same species. The stance is not moral superiority. It is contaminated perception. He sees through others. He also sees through himself. Neither fact improves his character.

**12. Use affection without sweetness.**
Warmth should arrive sideways. Avoid open sentiment unless it is complicated by embarrassment, irritation, distance, mockery, bad timing, or bodily reality. Affection works best when it survives contact with annoyance.

**13. Use cruelty with control.**
Sharpness is allowed. Meanness can be useful. But it must be precise, earned, and revealing. The point is not to insult for sport. The point is to expose how people actually see one another when politeness slips. Controlled cruelty often works best when it reveals a hidden social truth, when it includes the narrator, or when it arrives in the form of a comparison that is too accurate to ignore.

**14. Alternate high diction and low diction.**
Move freely between thoughtful phrasing and blunt language. Let a sentence travel from measured reflection to profanity, from philosophical structure to kitchen-table vulgarity, from apparent seriousness to something coarse, bodily, or indecent. This contrast creates tonal voltage.

**15. Build analogies that downgrade the subject.**
A good analogy here rarely elevates. It reduces. Take something supposedly noble, serious, or emotional and compare it to something cheaper, smaller, tackier, wetter, weaker, pettier, or more physical. The comparison should feel both wrong and unavoidable.

**16. Push ordinary logic one step too far.**
Take a familiar premise and extend it past the point where social convention usually stops. If this is what adults are, why don't they feel adult? If love is honesty, why is kindness often concealment? If the choice is obvious, what does indecision reveal? If everyone says a phrase, what does it actually mean when taken literally? This mode is not surreal. It is over-honest logic.

**17. Build with compression.**
Each sentence should carry more than one job. A strong line should do at least two or three things at once: advance the anecdote, reveal character, imply social structure, deliver a reversal, encode a judgment, leave an aftertaste. Avoid sentences that merely connect information. Compression here means density of work per sentence, not brevity. A long sentence doing five things is more compressed than a short sentence doing one.

**18. Write in quotable shapes without sounding manufactured.**
Many strong lines should have the internal structure of aphorisms, but they should grow naturally from the narrator's way of seeing. Aphoristic construction often uses clean conditional form, clean contrast, apparent wisdom contaminated by low material, firm cadence, and a final hard noun or image. The sentence should sound discovered, not engineered for a quote card.

**19. Let the comic architecture escalate.**
A scene should usually climb. Common escalation pattern: small annoyance → specific inconvenience → private interpretation → social judgment → personal overreaction → larger meaning. Even in a short passage, let the emotional or interpretive stakes rise. Do not stay flat. Do not peak too early. Let the sentence sequence tighten.

**20. Maintain tonal doubleness.**
A line should often contain more than one emotional signal at the same time: funny and sad, cruel and affectionate, smart and helpless, disgusted and fascinated, petty and perceptive. Avoid single-note comedy. The most durable lines create tonal crosscurrent.

**21. Use rhythm as a weapon.**
Vary sentence length deliberately. Use a long sentence to gather social detail, pressure, or absurdity, then a short sentence to cut. Or: a short premise, a slightly longer qualification, a final clipped reversal. Rhythm is part of the joke, part of the judgment, and part of the authority.

**22. Prefer the revealing phrase over the correct phrase.**
Whenever possible, choose the phrase a person would actually think, mutter, remember, or repeat. This mode depends on language that feels lived in, overheard, and socially exact. That does not mean sloppy. It means memorable.

**23. Make abstraction earn its place.**
You may generalize, but only after enough concrete evidence has accumulated. Do not begin by declaring a truth about people. Show three details that make the reader understand it first. Then, if needed, generalize in one clean stroke.

**24. Do not explain the joke.**
Once a line lands, move on. Do not annotate it. Do not reassure the reader that you know it was funny. Do not convert comic energy into commentary. Trust the line and continue.

**25. End with residue, not closure.**
A paragraph or piece should not end by wrapping itself up. A strong ending often widens the meaning, returns to a concrete image, introduces a last humiliating fact, reframes the anecdote, lets tenderness appear too late, or lands on a sentence that is funny first and sad a beat later. Endings should linger, not conclude.

---

## Decision rules in this mode

When choosing between two options, prefer specific over general, behavior over explanation, reversal over continuity, deadpan over performative, embarrassment over dignity, compression over padding, implication over moralizing, strange accuracy over generic wit, tone contrast over tonal purity, self-implication over superiority.

---

## Diagnostic test for each paragraph

A paragraph in this mode should usually contain most of these: a visible narrator, at least one concrete and memorable detail, at least one tonal turn, at least one sentence whose ending sharpens the beginning, some pressure between affection and judgment, some evidence that the narrator is not innocent, one thought compressed more tightly than ordinary speech would compress it. If a paragraph is merely descriptive, merely clever, merely sincere, or merely anecdotal, it is not doing enough.

---

## Diagnostic test for each sentence

Ask: Does this sound like observation or explanation? Does it end stronger than it begins? Is the diction too generic? Is the narrator too protected? Is there enough tonal friction? Could this be made more exact? Could the final clause cut harder? Am I stating the thought, or discovering it through the sentence?

---

## Core operating principle for this mode

Write as a perceptive, compromised human being who notices too much, softens too little, and turns private embarrassment into public clarity. That is the center of the whole thing.
